 A B S T R A C T
Fuzzy hashing can identify source code duplication in compiled binary functions, with applications ranging 
from vulnerability detection to malware attribution, where the source code is not available. However, 
its application across the compilation gap has not been systematically studied. This paper studies fuzzy 
hashes applied to cross-compiled Binary Function Similarity Analysis (BFSA). We first propose the Stratified 
Abstraction Model, a taxonomy that maps how platform divergence affects binary artefacts across six analysis 
layers. Then, we use it to classify existing approaches, ranging from learned embeddings to fuzzy hashes. 
Then, we evaluate the effect of divergence across compilers, optimisation settings, bitness, and architectures 
to reframe what Machine Learning techniques should be expected to achieve.
We conduct a large-scale evaluation of state-of-the-art fuzzy hashes, including Catalog1, Function-
SimSearch, Machoke, and CCBHash. We evaluate these techniques against uncompressed byte signatures 
(Position Independent Code), using a ground truth of cross-compiled functions, comprising 2.8 M targets and
4.3 M distractors. Under cross-architecture divergence (XA), the single-modality fuzzy hashes FCatalog and
Machoke reach 8.57E−4 and 0.0978 in Mean Reciprocal Rank at 10 (MRR@10). Conversely, the weighted 
multi-modal algorithms, CCBHash and FunctionSimSearch, achieve 0.4671 and 0.4720 in the same 
scenario. Under optimisation divergence (XO), CCBHash reaches the highest values, between 0.6271 with 
feature weights and 0.6865 with naive, unweighted features. Our results show that fuzzy hashes may be used 
to detect code duplication in cross-compiled binaries, where the original source code is not available. They 
cannot, however, detect different source implementations with shared intent. This second gap defines where 
learned representations become necessary.
1. Introduction
Binary code is the compiled output of human-readable source code 
and the ground truth of a program’s behaviour. Comparing compiled 
machine instructions without access to source code, a field known as 
Binary Code Similarity Analysis (BCSA), is a critical task in software 
security (Haq and Caballero, 2021; Marcelli et al., 2022; Kim et al., 
2023).
Modern vulnerability research (Pei et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a; 
Yang et al., 2021; Dullien, 2018) and malware attribution (Amalan 
et al., 2026; Rahat et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2020) require BCSA on 
cross-compiled binaries (Haq and Caballero, 2021). The challenge lies 
in the compilation gap: the divergence in low-level machine instructions 
produced from the same source code when compiled with different 
compilers, optimisation levels, or target architectures (Haq and Ca-
ballero, 2021; Marcelli et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2021). Similarly, the 
ISA and OS impose Application Binary Interfaces (ABIs) and calling 
conventions that binary analysis must account for Pang et al. (2021).

Analysis techniques must therefore look past compiler noise and 
platform-specific artefacts to recover the underlying program logic. 
Recent literature has gravitated towards Machine Learning (ML) tech-
niques for this task. However, ML approaches are not exempt from 
these challenges. These approaches frequently lift machine code into In-
termediate Representations (IRs) to overcome out-of-vocabulary degra-
dation (Wang et al., 2024a; Collyer et al., 2023; He et al., 2024). While 
an IR provides a unified vocabulary, it leaves trained models exposed 
to out-of-distribution degradation (Wang et al., 2024a; Collyer et al., 
2023; Marcelli et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). Furthermore, platform 
evolution and temporal concept drift compound additional shifts in 
feature distributions, degrading malware classification performance in 
ML models after deployment (Ma et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021a,b). 
Malware binaries are harder to analyse by design. Threat actors de-
feat classifiers with perturbations (Demetrio et al., 2021; Uhm et al., 
2026) or bypass analysis environments with anti-debugging techniques

Compounding these challenges, there is the need for continuous la-
belling and retraining of ML classifiers to maintain accuracy as malware 
evolves (Pendlebury et al., 2019). Furthermore, they are systematically 
evaluated under biased synthetic conditions that overstate their prac-
tical viability (Arp et al., 2024). Concretely, models trained on one 
organisation’s corpus reflect that organisation’s threat distribution and 
may learn dataset-specific patterns rather than transferable security 
signals (Arp et al., 2024).
In those edge cases where ML techniques may struggle, fuzzy hashes 
offer a promising alternative, hashing features into a universal vo-
cabulary that is agnostic to training-induced concept drift (Onieva 
et al., 2024; Dullien, 2018; ANSSI, 2015; xorpd, 2026). Given a fixed 
reverse-engineering toolchain that recovers consistent features, the 
same binary will always produce the same hash. This determinism does 
not apply equally in ML models. Namely, after model retraining, deep 
learning embeddings must be kept up to date with the model weight 
parameters by regenerating the complete dataset (Gagnon et al., 2025). 
Furthermore, studies have shown that simpler approaches can match 
the accuracy of these models, proving that BCSA researchers require 
meaningful baselines to measure ML progress (Marcelli et al., 2022; 
Kim et al., 2023; Saul et al., 2024). These advantages, however, may 
come at the cost of lower performance, as they often tie the hash to 
compilation-induced noise and boilerplate that misleads code similarity 
detection efforts.
However, in those edge cases where ML does not have sufficiently 
representative training datasets, such as zero-day or cross-compiled 
malware, fuzzy hashing approaches have demonstrated preliminary 
potential (Costin et al., 2014; Tan, 2021; Onieva et al., 2024). De-
spite this, the following question remains unanswered: To what extent 
do traditional fuzzy hashes support binary code similarity analysis on 
cross-compiled binaries?
To the best of our knowledge, this question has not been system-
atically studied. We formalise our research objectives as five research 
questions in Section 2. We emphasise that this paper does not seek to 
compare the performance of fuzzy hashing versus ML-based approaches 
or argue for the superiority of either. Instead, our objective is to 
investigate the capabilities and limitations of fuzzy hashing in cross-
compiled settings and to examine how it can complement ML-based 
representations.
1.1. Contributions
This paper studies fuzzy hashing and empirically evaluates its vi-
ability for Binary Function Similarity Analysis (BFSA) tasks in cross-
compiled settings. We also propose the Stratified Binary Abstraction 
Model, which maps how compiler, Operating System (OS), and architec-
tural divergences mutate the artefacts recovered at different depths of 
analysis. We design this model to apply to any BFSA technique beyond 
fuzzy hashes.
Next, we conduct the first large-scale evaluation of modern fuzzy 
hashes in cross-compiled settings, utilising radare2 to maximise re-
producibility. We infer that compiler divergence affects features across 
abstraction layers differently, explaining why CCBHash recall rates 
surpass FunctionSimSearch.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• The Stratified Binary Abstraction Model: We propose a layered 
taxonomy that categorises features extracted from binary analysis 
artefacts, mapping how divergence affects feature recovery at each 
layer of abstraction. (Section 3)
• Review of BFSA algorithms: We review the literature, classifying 
existing approaches with the Stratified Binary Abstraction Model to 
highlight constraints across the vulnerability search and malware 
attribution domains. (Section 4)
• BinRankBench Framework: We release our evaluation frame-
work with the pre-processed binary artefacts, features, and fuzzy-
hash database. (Section 6.2)
• Fuzzy Hashing Evaluation: We provide the first large-scale eval-
uation of function-level fuzzy hashes, measuring their recall and 
robustness across the compilation gap. (Section 7)
2. Preliminaries and problem domain
Problem formulation. The reconstruction of binary code logic is not 
a monolithic task but a stratified process of semantic recovery. Be-
cause human-readable languages are translated into executable bina-
ries through compilation, the resulting sequential stream of low-level 
machine instructions suffers from a significant loss of information.
When the compiler processes high-level abstractions such as original 
code structure, comments, variables, and function names, the resulting 
executable is transformed substantially depending on the execution 
environment and compiler settings, obscuring the original logic.
In the absence of source code, such as in proprietary software or 
malware, opaque binary representations are the only available source 
of information. Consequently, the semantic comparison of a program’s 
original logic becomes a multi-stage pipeline: reconstructing artefacts 
through reverse engineering, normalising divergent features, and dis-
tilling them into compressed, comparable, and accurate fingerprints.
Research objectives. This paper systematises the divergence of compiled 
binary artefacts and reviews the available techniques for analysing 
the similarity of cross-compiled binaries when the source code is not 
available. The wider literature uses the term ‘‘cross-platform’’ to denote 
binary code divergence across Instruction Set Architectures (ISAs) and 
OSs, while the term ‘‘cross-compiler’’ normally envelops the choice of 
compiler toolchains and compilation settings.
We adopt the term ‘‘cross-compilation’’ to describe all sources of 
binary code divergence, arising both from the compilation environment 
(choice of compiler and optimisation settings) and the target system 
(ISA or OS1). Binaries that exhibit this divergence are referred to as
cross-compiled throughout.
Research questions. Having identified compilation divergence as our 
focus, we outline the following research questions:
RQ1. How does compilation divergence propagate across the features 
recovered from binary code? (Section 3)
RQ2. What are the different techniques to process and compress bi-
nary features for BFSA? (Section 4)
RQ3. How do binary-function hashes extend their design beyond the 
scope of traditional fuzzy hashes? (Section 5)
RQ4. Can binary-function fuzzy hashes approximate similarity across 
the compilation gap? (Section 7.1)
RQ5. How does the computational efficiency of binary-function fuzzy 
hashes measure in terms of digest footprint and practical
throughput? (Section 7.2).

3. The stratified abstraction of binary artefacts
Cross-platform BCSA approaches vary widely in both feature se-
lection and similarity approximation strategy. The accuracy of any 
given approach is constrained by the fidelity of the selected features 
in representing the underlying program logic.
To address RQ1, we propose the Stratified Binary Abstraction Model, 
summarised in Table  1 and illustrated in Fig.  1. This framework cat-
egorises recoverable binary artefacts from the raw file surface (L0) to 
dynamic behaviour (L5). At the same time, we mapped the primary 
drivers of compilation divergence to the specific layers where they man-
ifest as analytical challenges, covering compiler toolchain, optimisation 
settings, architecture family, and bitness (Cooper and Torczon, 2012).
Cross-compiled code similarity approximation extends beyond the 
design assumptions of traditional fuzzy hashing algorithms matching 
general byte sequences and files (Kornblum, 2006; Oliver et al., 2013; 
Raff and Nicholas, 2018; Roussev, 2011, 2010). Binary code is more 
nuanced: raw bytes encode low-level instructions that computers pro-
cess deterministically, while following a structural and logical flow that 
is influenced by the machine state during live execution. Therefore, 
we argue that understanding how raw bytes can be analysed to infer 
execution behaviour is essential for designing effective BCSA strategies. 
In particular, fuzzy hashes must bridge the compilation gap without 
relying on representation learning approaches.
This model systematises the artefacts recovered from binary code 
to provide a descriptive framework of features and their resilience 
to the compilation gap without pinning a specific BFSA technique or 
reverse-engineering algorithm. Sections 4 and 7 use it to place heuristic 
signatures, ML embeddings, and fuzzy hashes on a shared analytical 
footing.
Fig.  1 groups reverse-engineered features into three domains: Lexi-
cal, Structural, and Operational, sharing similar representations, such as 
linear sequences, graphs, or state-transformation models, respectively. 
This high-level grouping provides a rationale for feature selection based 
on data modality and the depth of execution recovery.
We align the BCSA paradigms covered in this review with their 
choice of features and required depth of analysis. The procedural 
nuances and heuristics involved in binary analysis vary widely across 
toolchains. For a comprehensive review of these algorithmic challenges, 
we refer the reader to the work by Pang et al. (2021).
We present our model linearly to reflect increasing computational 
and abstraction costs, with the central spine representing feature hi-
erarchy. To reflect the heuristic and iterative nature of real-world

3.1. Lexical domain
The Lexical domain encompasses the statically observable proper-
ties of the binary before any structural grouping. It consists of the 
Surface (L0) and Syntax (L1) layers, which share a sequential modality.
In this domain, the binary is treated as a linear stream of atomic 
symbols, such as bytes or mnemonics (L0, L1). These layers are suitable 
for the rapid extraction of features and subsequent analysis with Nat-
ural Language Processing (NLP)-inspired methods (Ding et al., 2019; 
Saul et al., 2024), albeit at the cost of ignoring logic flow.
L0: Surface. The Surface layer treats the binary as a structured data 
container, governed by specifications such as Portable Executable (PE) 
and Executable Linkable Format (ELF) (Levine, 1999). Artefacts in-
clude raw byte sequences, file headers, and metadata such as exported 
symbols or embedded strings.
While computationally trivial and suitable for rapid byte-level sig-
nature matching, L0 is highly brittle: metadata is routinely stripped 
in production binaries. Compiler optimisations and packing compress 
code into surface data with high Shannon entropy.
L0 is also highly sensitive to cross-OS and architectural divergence. 
Artefacts such as header configurations and system library interfaces 
(ABIs) differ across OS environments, while bitness and data models 
(e.g. x86 vs. x64) alter memory addressing limits and pointer sizes, 
causing immediate byte-level divergence for identical logic.
L1: Syntax. At the Syntax layer, raw streams are translated into ma-
chine instructions via disassembly, consisting of mnemonics, operands, 
and immediate values. This represents the binary as a sequence of 
atomic operations mandated by the specific ISA. The primary limitation 
here is the total lack of cross-architecture transferability. Because the 
vocabulary is bound to the ISA, syntactically divergent streams rou-
tinely express identical intent. For example, a single multi-effect Com-
plex Instruction Set Computer (CISC) instruction may express the same 
intent as multiple simpler load/store operations in a Reduced Instruc-
tion Set Computer (RISC) ISA. Additionally, differing compiler instruc-
tion selection heuristics tile operations into diverse machine instructions 
(Cooper and Torczon, 2012, Ch. 11).
Beyond architecture divergence, the challenge of code-data disam-
biguation remains, causing disassemblers to misinterpret inline data 
as junk instructions (Andriesse et al., 2016). This layer also relies 
entirely on identifying the correct Original Entry Point (OEP). The use 
of packers and ‘‘stolen bytes’’ in malware hides this starting point, 
often tricking L1 tools into decoding the unpacking stub rather than 
the actual malicious payload (Kim et al., 2019).
3.2. Structural domain
The Structural domain moves beyond linear sequences to model the 
connectivity and the logical boundaries of the code, encompassing the 
Topology (L2) and Scope (L3) layers. The rationale for this grouping is 
the shift towards relational modality.
In this domain, the primary features are represented as graphs, 
where the edges reflect possible execution paths. Specifically, nodes 
represent either basic blocks (sequences of branch-free instructions) to 
form an intra-procedural Control Flow Graph (CFG), or entire functions 
to reconstruct the global Call Graph (CG) of the binary file (L2, L3).
L2: Topology. The Topology layer abstracts the instruction stream 
into a local graph-based representation, typically the CFG or the 
inter-procedural CFG (ICFG), which model flow between basic blocks 
(maximal-length sequences of branch-free instructions).
However, this structure is also affected by optimisation and archi-
tectural divergence (Andriesse et al., 2016). Compilers aggressively pri-
oritise execution efficiency by altering the CFG via dead code elimina-
tion, loop unrolling, and code motion, which redistributes instructions 
across basic blocks (Cooper and Torczon, 2012, Ch. 10).
Differing condition models (e.g. status flags and predicated exe-
cution) and micro-architectural instruction scheduling further mutate 
the CFG (Cifuentes and Van Emmerik, 2000; Hennessy and Patterson, 
2017). For instance, predicated execution allows compilers to eliminate 
branch instructions, collapsing multiple conditional paths into a single 
basic block and altering the topological structure of nodes and edges.
L3: Scope. The Scope layer partitions the global graph into discrete 
functional units and their inter-procedural relationships, described by 
a CG. The inter-procedural CFG represents the global program graph
before functional decomposition. Recovering the function boundaries 
that partition the Inter-procedural Control Flow Graph (ICFG) into in-
dividual CFGs is itself a Scope-layer operation. Approaches that model 
inter-procedural call edges as explicit features operate at L3.
This layer captures modular intent but is highly susceptible to 
structural rearrangements and boundary detection failures (Rosen-
blum et al., 2008). Optimisation divergence heavily fragments the 
CG through procedure cloning (specialising functions for specific con-
texts) and function inlining (dissolving procedure boundaries entirely) 
(Cooper and Torczon, 2012, Ch. 8). Furthermore, varying compiler 
linkage implementations and ABIs, such as differing prologue/epilogue 
generation and tail-call optimisations, obscure the boundary between 
intra-procedural topology and inter-procedural scope.
3.3. Operational domain
The Operational domain closes the gap between platform-specific 
binary code and the underlying machine operations it encodes by mod-
elling execution semantics, consisting of the Logic (L4) and Behaviour 
(L5) layers. These layers address a shared objective: to isolate the func-
tional ‘‘truth’’ of the code from its lexical and structural manifestations. 
Unlike previous domains, the Operational domain focuses on state-
transformation modality. Artefacts describe how a program transforms 
processor state, rather than what its code looks like structurally or 
lexically.
Within this domain, the boundary between L4 and L5 is determined 
by whether the binary is executed. L4 arises from static analysis: 
lifting instructions to an architecture-neutral IR, constructing Data Flow 
Graphs (DFGs), or applying symbolic execution to enumerate possible 
states through constraint solving; all without running the binary. L5 
arises when instructions are executed, whether on bare metal, in a vir-
tual machine, or in an emulation engine. Concolic execution straddles 
both: the concrete execution component generates runtime values (L5) 
that seed and prune the symbolic constraint solver (L4).
This execution boundary also governs how dynamically derived ana-
logues of lower-layer artefacts are classified. Raw bytes and instruction 
sequences from dynamic execution, such as memory dumps and micro-
traces, are L5 rather than L0 or L1, as they capture a path-dependent 
subset of the binary’s instruction vocabulary rather than its complete 
view. Conversely, graph-structured artefacts such as dynamic basic 
block coverage or function call traces are L5 rather than L2 or L3, as 
they reflect only the paths traversed during a specific run rather than 
the complete topology of the binary.

L4: Logic. The Logic layer decouples features from the underlying 
architecture by lifting instructions into semantically richer represen-
tations. This layer spans a wide abstraction spectrum: at the lower 
end, low-level IRs such as Evaluable Strings Intermediate Language 
(ESIL), VEX-IR, and P-Code model per-instruction state transitions 
(hidden flag updates, register side-effects, memory semantics) inde-
pendently of the ISA (Shoshitaishvili et al., 2016). At the higher end, 
decompiled representations such as Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) and 
DFGs recover program-level logical structure. All artefacts in this layer 
share architecture-neutral representations and are recovered without 
executing the binary.
While IRs normalise the ISA, they do not resolve compiler di-
vergence. Differing register allocation strategies and IR philosophies, 
such as Static Single Assignment (SSA) and Register Transfer Language 
(RTL), dictate the initial structure and memory traffic, with varying 
register pressure affecting the frequency of stack ‘‘spill code’’ generated 
(Cooper and Torczon, 2012, Ch. 5, 13).
Compiler optimisations, such as constant folding, strength reduc-
tion, and redundancy elimination, also substantially alter the resulting 
structure. As a result, identical constructs lower into vastly different IR 
patterns depending on both the compiler toolchain and optimisation 
flags. To reach a unified semantic state, higher abstractions such as 
concrete and symbolic (concolic) execution are used to enrich symbolic 
program logic (L4) with concrete program states (L5) (Godefroid et al., 
2005; Sen et al., 2005).
L5: Behaviour . The Behaviour layer represents the highest level of 
abstraction, derived from observing the program’s effects during execu-
tion. Methodologies here range from capturing memory dumps during 
virtualised or bare-metal execution to extracting dynamic IR traces via 
emulation and solving path constraints through concolic execution.
While capturing what the code concretely does improves resilience 
to compiler optimisations and static obfuscation (Song et al., 2008), 
L5 features are not immune to platform divergence. Artefacts such 
as dynamic execution traces and system call sequences vary sharply 
across platforms; an identical high-level operation will yield a POSIX-
compliant system call trace on Linux but invoke a completely distinct
Win32 or NTAPI sequence on Windows. Similarly, architectural diver-
gence in bitness and data models significantly affects memory mutation 
patterns.
The choice of L5 methodology also bifurcates based on the an-
alytical objective, introducing further variability into the recovered 
artefacts. In malware clustering and attribution, analysts frequently 
encounter dynamic evasion tactics, where malicious binaries alter or 
halt their execution upon detecting debuggers or artificial analysis 
environments. These scenarios typically require high-fidelity methods 
like hardware-in-the-loop or bare-metal execution. Conversely, cross-
compiled vulnerability discovery rarely contends with intentional en-
vironment evasion. Therefore, analysts carry out these tasks with emu-
lation or concolic execution. While susceptible to anti-analysis tactics, 
these techniques facilitate the verification of bugs through exhaustive 
state-space exploration and path constraint solving. Thus, L5 analy-
sis recovers artefacts inaccessible to static analysis. Dynamic insights 
gained here, such as indirect jump targets resolved with emulation, are 
fed back to lower layers to refine the entire reconstruction process.
3.4. Cross-layer dynamics
As shown in Fig.  1, real-world analysis often employs non-linear 
paths. Abstraction Bypasses (blue) represent streamlining heuristics, 
such as using byte-level patterns to identify function prologues (L0 to 
L3) or employing native execution traces to bypass complex static graph 
reconstruction.
Conversely, Iterative Refinements (red) facilitate feedback loops 
that resolve ambiguity. For example, resolving computed jump targets 
at the Logic layer (L4) or via Behavioural emulation (L5) can populate 
previously ‘‘opaque’’ edges in the Topology (L2). This feedback loop is 
essential for code-data disambiguation, where dynamic syntax recovery 
feeds code unpacked at runtime back into the analysis pipeline to 
maximise coverage.
The accuracy of recovered binary artefacts depends on the choice 
of extraction toolchains and recovery settings, producing coverage-vs-
correctness trade-offs. Pang et al. (2021) discuss code interpretation er-
rors and coverage shortcomings across reverse-engineering algorithms 
in detail.
3.5. Relationship between fuzzy hashing and the proposed taxonomy
The stratified binary abstraction model is not limited to fuzzy 
hashes; rather, it is a framework for categorising features recovered by 
disassemblers (known as artefacts) based on their sensitivity to compi-
lation divergence. Large-scale Binary Code Similarity Analysis (BCSA), 
whether focused on function-level or program-level similarity, relies 
on various methods, such as fuzzy hashes, tensor-based embeddings, 
or representation-learning approaches (Marcelli et al., 2022; Haq and 
Caballero, 2021; Wang et al., 2024b). These methods have traditionally 
used simple features such as call graphs, edge counts from control flow 
graphs, and other features that capture the rich semantics of binary 
data.
Certain features, particularly structure-based ones, can be signifi-
cantly affected by different compilation environments and variants of 
the same binary program, whether it is benign or malicious software 
(Ding et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2025). Recently, fuzzy hashing algorithms 
have found applications in cross-architecture binary search, cross-build 
binary datasets, as well as in evaluating code similarity across differ-
ent optimisation and compiler environments, using features that have 
been currently mapped to the proposed stratified abstraction layer 
such as LLVM IR (Intermediate Representation), function call graphs, 
bytes, Abstract Syntax Trees, strings, mnemonics and operands (Onieva 
et al., 2024; Tan, 2021; Mahmoudyar et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022; 
Liu et al., 2026; Dullien, 2018). It is therefore important to deter-
mine which features can enhance the effectiveness of fuzzy hashing 
in cross-optimisation, cross-compiler, cross-architecture, and cross-OS 
BCSA.
Furthermore, traditional fuzzy hashing algorithms, such as SS-
DEEP, TLSH, sdhash, and LZJD, were not originally designed for 
cross-platform BCSA, motivating an investigation into which additional 
features could be incorporated to extend their applicability beyond 
their original scope. For instance, fuzzy hashing algorithms such as
SSDEEP, TLSH, Permhash, ImpHash, and Telfhash are already 
employed as baseline malware properties to provide similarity metrics 
for threat-hunting investigations on VirusTotal (VirusTotal, 2020; Mar-
tinez, 2023; Merces, 2020). Subsequent studies grounded in a binary 
abstraction model could help clarify which features are most pertinent 
to cross-platform malware similarity analysis and threat hunting across 
heterogeneous devices and systems.
3.6. Cross-platform BFSA and the original design assumptions of fuzzy 
hashing
While it is true that many classical fuzzy hashing algorithms were 
not originally designed for cross-platform BCSA, most of the algorithms 
examined in this study were developed to address compiler and optimi-
sation divergence. Position Independent Code (PIC) strips away bytes 
subject to address relocations, CCBHash was specifically designed to be 
resilient against compiler optimisations, and FunctionSimSearch
aimed to provide resilience against both optimisations and compil-
ers. However, neither of these algorithms considered resilience across 
different architectures and bitness.
Moreover, existing literature has demonstrated the application of 
classical fuzzy hashing algorithms in cross-platform analysis, high-
lighting the need to evaluate fuzzy hashing as a means of bridging

the compilation gap. For instance, Tan (2021) lifted function code 
into IRs and hashed these with classical fuzzy hashes (such as Min-

been evaluated under the considerably more demanding conditions of 
cross-architecture semantic equivalence, providing validation for this 
line of research.
4. Cross-platform binary similarity analysis: Systematisation of 
the state of the art
The accuracy and scalability constraints of the domains BCSA serve 
to define two distinct design spaces. This section addresses RQ2 by nar-
rowing the scope of our review to function-level matching (Section 4.1) 
and examining how domain-specific requirements influence the design 
of vulnerability detection and malware attribution techniques (Sections 4.2
and 4.3).
4.1. Design constraints and review rationale
Vulnerability detection vs. Malware attribution. The design of a BCSA 
system is shaped by its target domain, which typically bifurcates into 
software security and digital forensics. These domains are exemplified 
by vulnerability detection and malware attribution, respectively. In 
vulnerability detection, BCSA techniques seek high semantic precision 
to identify N-day vulnerabilities across platforms, such as in cross-
compiled Internet of Things (IoT) firmware. This requires scalable, 
1-to-N retrieval mechanisms capable of abstracting away diverse ISAs 
and compiler optimisations.
Malware analysis, by contrast, operates in an adversarial space. 
Rather than exact matches, these tasks rely on similarity metrics to 
cluster obfuscated variants, allowing analysts to bypass intentional 
structural mutations and reconstruct the evolutionary lineage of a 
threat actor’s operations.
The granularity vs. Scalability trade-off. Both domains operate under 
the constraint of missing source code. Analysts must extract relational 
patterns, reconstruct a program’s logic, and estimate similarity entirely 
from machine code. This process is governed by the granularity of the 
extracted features and the matching methodology (Javed et al., 2025).
Features may range from high-level functional attributes, such as 
variable counts and linkage dependencies, to atomic instruction-level 
details like mnemonics and immediate values. Likewise, similarity ap-
proximations can be calculated at the level of discrete functions or 
across entire binary objects.
Depending on the practical use case, the granularities of feature 
selection and similarity estimation establish the trade-off between the
depth of analysis and the scalability of comparison models. There-
fore, practical constraints, such as the scale of analysis, determine the 
different design choices of BCSA methods.
Focus of this review. Given these constraints and the extensive pre-
vious work covering broad BCSA domains (Ruan et al., 2024; Javed 
et al., 2025), our review focuses on cross-compiled BFSA, a subset of 
BCSA aiming to identify matches within discrete functional units of 
executable files against large-scale ground truth databases.
We select function-level granularity to strike a balance between 
providing sufficient context to identify shared logic across diverse 
binaries, while avoiding the computational bottleneck of precise, 1-to-1 
analysis techniques (Google, 2011; Joxean, 2015).
To this end, we consider two primary use cases of BFSA: (a) the 
detection of vulnerable components to facilitate downstream patching 
in cross-compiled environments (such as IoT firmware), and (b) the 
attribution of binary samples to specific malware actors based on code 
duplication arising from iterative development and plagiarism of public 
repositories (Gamblin, 2016) (see Table  2 and Table  3).
4.2. Vulnerability detection in cross-platform binaries
The detection of known vulnerabilities within cross-compiled envi-
ronments, such as IoT firmware, relies on functional matching against 
a ground-truth dataset of binaries affected by known vulnerabilities.
However, the implementation of these systems is dictated by the 
underlying platform scope and the scale of binaries to be searched. The 
primary challenge in this domain is balancing the semantic accuracy 
required to see through compilation divergence with the computational 
constraints of querying large-scale repositories.
4.2.1. Accurate 1-to-1 diffing vs. Scalable 1-to-N indexing
Heuristic diffing. Early approaches to vulnerability and patch analysis 
spanned the Syntax (L1) and Structural (L2–L3) layers, relying on graph 
isomorphism heuristics over disassembled code to identify differences 
in CFGs and CGs.
Tools such as BinDiff and Diaphora (Google, 2011; Joxean, 
2015) excel at 1-to-1 comparison, providing highly accurate, localised 
differences in disassembled code. However, the computational com-
plexity of graph matching introduces a severe scalability bottleneck, 
rendering these tools unviable for 1-to-N searches across large firmware 
repositories.
Scalable hashing. To achieve true 1-to-N scalability, initial repository-
scale search engines were forced to drop down to the Lexical (L0–L1) 
and Topology (L2) layers, prioritising rapid querying over semantic 
depth by extracting byte-level signatures (Radare2 Team, 2014) or CFG 
hashes (Ding et al., 2016).
A seminal example of scalable topological indexing is the initial 
iteration of the Kam1n0 engine, which introduced MapReduce, an 
Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) scheme operating on the assembly 
code of CFG sub-graphs (L1–L2). Rendezvous (Khoo et al., 2013) tar-
gets the same Topology layer (L2) through sub-graph canonicalisation. 
We further discuss this canonicalisation technique in Section 5.1.3.
Another influential approach to scalable retrieval is Function-
SimSearch (Dullien, 2018). This tool adapts the SimHash algorithm 
(Sadowski and Levin, 2007) to approximate the angular similarity 
between binary functions. By projecting mnemonic trigrams (L1), im-
mediate values (L1), and CFG graphlets (L2) into a compact 128-bit 
hash, it enables extremely fast 1-to-N nearest-neighbour searches via 
Hamming distance.
FunctionSimSearch applies adaptive weighting, assigning
higher importance to immediate values than to graphlets and mnemon-
ics. This multi-modality improves resilience to cross-compiler diver-
gence while maintaining the low computational overhead needed for 
large-scale repositories. FuzzyBin (Kim et al., 2025) extends function-
level fuzzy hashing to identify IoT firmware handling external user 
input (border binaries) by comparing normalised assembly hashes 
against a reference and applying bipartite matching to produce a 
whole-binary similarity score.

Sequential embeddings. At the lower abstraction layers, available fea-
tures are heavily shaped by the syntactic divergence introduced by 
different compiler toolchains. Therefore, Asm2Vec (Ding et al., 2019) 
replaced hash-based indexing with a representation learning model 
designed to capture the latent lexical semantics of assembly operations 
via random walks on the CFG.
Similarly, SAFE (Massarelli et al., 2022) adopts self-attentive neural 
networks to generate function embeddings directly from disassembled 
code, prioritising computational efficiency across different architec-
tures. This paradigm was further advanced by jTrans (Wang et al., 
2022), which employs a ‘‘jump-aware’’ Transformer to explicitly model 
the structural relationships of the CFG alongside the semantics of 
assembly instructions. Taking an unsupervised approach, Codee (Yang 
et al., 2022) arranges per-block embeddings derived from network 
representation learning of ICFG into a three-dimensional tensor and 
applied truncated singular value decomposition to produce compact, 
fixed-length function vectors without requiring labelled training data.
While these models achieve semantic capture via deep representa-
tion learning, they remain bound to the machine-level vocabulary of the 
ISAs seen during training, making them susceptible to concept drift. As 
digital systems continuously evolve, the limited availability of ground 
truth for niche or emerging architectures leads to out-of-vocabulary 
tokens and previously unseen sequences at the syntax layer that prevent 
accurate generalisation to cross-compiled binaries.
4.2.2. Embedding operational artefacts for semantic search
Semantic embeddings. To resolve the vocabulary drift and architectural 
dependence of syntax-bound methods, modern research has ascended 
to the Logic layer (L4) through representation learning. The core inno-
vation here is the decoupling of program logic from hardware-specific 
implementations by lifting instructions into an architecture-neutral IR. 
By normalising disparate ISAs into a unified intermediate language, 
analysts can mitigate the variance introduced by mnemonic vocabular-
ies and register allocation, narrowing the compilation gap that hinders 
assembly-based models.
Deep learning models transform IRs into dense numerical vectors 
(embeddings), translating the computationally prohibitive task of graph 
isomorphism into a scalable vector distance calculation, which enables 
Approximate Nearest Neighbour (ANN) search for repository-scale re-
trieval and improves accuracy across diverging platforms.
For example, FASER (Collyer et al., 2023) demonstrates that lifting 
binaries into an IR (Radare2’s ESIL) provides a standardised string 
representation for Transformer-based models. Similarly, VexIR2Vec
(VenkataKeerthy et al., 2025) uses VEX-IR to extract operational em-
beddings, showing that L4 abstractions provide more reliable features 
than L1 for cross-architecture matching.
Graph embeddings. Sequential models provide some resilience to com-
pilation divergence but ignore the structural semantics of code. Graph-
based representation learning addresses this through a relational
modality. This is primarily achieved through two distinct approaches.
A substantial body of prior research represents functions as At-
tributed Control Flow Graphs (ACFGs). These methods extract an ad-
jacency matrix from the structural CFG (L2) and enrich individual 
nodes with instruction-level statistics (L1), such as counts of arithmetic 
operations, memory accesses, and branch instructions.
Namely, DGIS (Dual-Granularity Interactive Semantic Learning),
GMN (Graph-Matching Network), DiscovRE, Gemini, and
VulSeeker (Liu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Eschweiler et al., 2016; 
Xu et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2018) survive aggressive compiler optimi-
sations by grounding graph structures in numerical node attributes, 
leveraging deep learning to recognise semantically equivalent binary 
code. GMN explicitly follows FunctionSimSearch (Dullien, 2018)

in its feature selection, adopting the same L1–L2 combination of as-
sembly instruction embeddings and CFG structure, while replacing the 
hand-engineered graph hash with a learned graph matching network.
Building upon this need for structural context, Collyer et al. (2024) 
introduced the concept of Call Graphlets to capture the ‘‘neighbour-
hood’’ context of a function. This approach characterises functions 
using a multi-layered feature set including L1 instruction counts, L2 
CFG edge density, L3 the function’s in-degree, out-degree, and callee 
out-degree in the CG, and L4 the number of recovered local variables 
and arguments.
Subsequent work has extended the range of graph structures used 
for function representation. Asteria (Yang et al., 2021) replaces the 
L2 CFG with the L4 AST to encode hierarchical syntactic structure 
independently of architecture-specific mnemonics. Kim et al. (2022) ab-
stract binaries as Binary Disassembly Graphs (BDGs), encoding control-
flow alongside contextual information such as external calls and refer-
enced string literals, and formulate cross-compiled matching as a graph 
alignment problem solved with Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs).
Focus (Fu et al., 2022) applies a Graph Neural Network (GNN) with 
multi-head attention to identify discriminative structural features for 
cross-architecture clone detection. Uhlig et al. (2023) treated fuzzy 
hashes (TLSH, SSDEEP) computed from L0 raw file bytes as sequences 
that can be tokenized and fed into a transformer network to improve 
fragment-to-file matching in digital forensics.
Execution semantics. While these sequential and graph-based represen-
tations provide significant resilience against static variance, they still 
operate on a reconstruction of the code. The state of the art has further 
refined this by learning behavioural semantics directly from execution. 
For instance, TREX (Pei et al., 2023) learns approximate execution 
semantics from micro-traces (L5), capturing what the code actually does
at the register and memory level rather than just how it is structured.
Further approaches ground matching in semantic normalisation 
or symbolic equivalence. Pewny et al. (2015) addressed the com-
pilation gap by introducing one of the first bug search algorithms 
for cross-architecture binaries. Their proposal involved translating as-
sembly code into VEX representations (L4) to deduce semantically 
equivalent expressions at basic-block granularity (L2), based on con-
crete input/output behaviour. HermesSim (He et al., 2024) repre-
sents functions as semantics-oriented graphs over a simplified IR (L4), 
normalising away architecture-specific instruction vocabulary while 
retaining the underlying CFG topology (L2). Luo et al. (2017) likewise 
combine L2 basic-block topology with static L4 symbolic execution, 
computing the longest common subsequence of basic blocks and testing 
semantic equivalence via a constraint solver. KEENHash (Liu et al., 
2025) generated LLM-based function embeddings from Ghidra pseu-
docode and ASTs (L4), combined with a hashing scheme for efficient 
large-scale binary-function similarity search.
This logic anchoring allows analysts to see through minor obfusca-
tions, such as the ones resulting from compiler divergence. However, 
static analysis cannot overcome more aggressive packing techniques 
like virtualisation. Moreover, the storage and execution time needed 
to extract and compare symbolic execution traces across millions of 
functions make direct searches over concrete operational features in-
tractable at scale.
Hybrid pre-filtering. To balance scalable searches and precise analysis, 
previous work has adopted tiered pre-filtering. This hierarchical ap-
proach has historical precedent in tools like BinGo (Chandramohan 
et al., 2016), which pre-filtered candidates with lightweight features 
(library call names, instruction type distributions) before applying sym-
bolic expression extraction and Z3 constraint solving (L4) to compare 
the input/output semantics of candidate functions.
Another example is CrossDiff (Wang et al., 2019), which uses 
architecture-neutral IRs (LLVM (Lattner and Adve, 2004)) to identify 
identical L4 matches via cryptographic hashing and likely candidates 
through SimHash pre-filtering, reserving expensive metrics like the 
longest common sub-string for final confidence scoring.
Similarly, CEBin (Wang et al., 2024a) lifts functions to BinaryNinja 
Intermediate Language (Vector 35, 2026) and applies this L4 repre-
sentation to both an embedding-based pre-filter and a fine-grained 
pairwise comparison model, capturing structural relationships that a 
single embedding might miss.
Several L4 approaches share a common strategy: IR lifting for cross-
ISA normalisation, followed by re-tokenisation of the resulting IR as an 
assembly-like token vocabulary. CEBin and CIAO (Rahat et al., 2026) 
both apply this approach: CEBin over Binary Ninja’s intermediate lan-
guage, CIAO over Radare2’s ESIL. Both decouple the token vocabulary 
from the target ISA whilst preserving instruction-level granularity.
This tiered architecture allows systems to apply computationally ex-
pensive comparison schemes without sacrificing the scalability required 
for supply-chain vulnerability discovery.
4.3. Malware clustering and authorship attribution
While vulnerability detection typically seeks exact or near-exact 
functional matches to patch known bugs, malware authorship attribu-
tion operates in a highly adversarial space. Here, the primary objective 
is to link unknown, obfuscated, or polymorphic samples to specific 
threat actors, malware families, or known code bases.
This domain imposes unique constraints on BCSA: the analysis must 
see through intentional obfuscation strategies aimed at evading detec-
tion (like packing and encryption) and provide explainable, forensic 
evidence that two disparate payloads share a development lineage 
based on reused components.
4.3.1. Macroscopic malware detection and classification
In practical incident response, analysts face large volumes of poly-
morphic variants. Historically, triaging these samples has relied on 
file-level indicators at the Surface layer (L0).
Imports and permissions hashing. Algorithms such as ImpHash, Imp-
Fuzzy, or TELFHash (Mandiant, 2014; JPCERT, 2017; Mercês and 
Costoya, 2020) capture the external dependencies of a binary. Recently, 
this Surface-layer heuristic approach has been expanded by frameworks 
like Permhash, which hashes the declared permissions of Chromium-
based browser extensions and Android Package Kit (APK) applications 
to map out threat campaigns and cluster adversary toolkits (Wilson, 
2023; Martinez, 2023).
File byte hashing. Alongside these indicator hashes, file-level fuzzy 
hashes like SSDEEP, TLSH, and SDHash (Kornblum, 2006; Oliver 
et al., 2013; Roussev, 2010) evaluate the macroscopic byte structure. 
More recent advances in this space, such as the Lempel-Ziv Jaccard 
Distance (LZJD) (Raff and Nicholas, 2018), have emerged as highly 
efficient alternatives. By representing a file as a set of sub-strings 
and approximating Jaccard distance, LZJD provides significantly faster 
comparison times and improved resistance to random noise insertion 
and fragmentation. Khan and Al-Awami (2026) demonstrate that fixed-
threshold classification with SSDEEP, TLSH, and LZJD can detect ELF 
IoT malware with no model training and low memory footprint.
ML-based classification. While malware analysis platforms like Virus-
Total (VirusTotal, 2026) report on pure hashing metrics for broad 
clustering, surface-level features have been applied to ML approaches. 
Notably, EMBER features (Şandor et al., 2023) are widely used for static 
malware detection and classification. Furthermore, traditional fuzzy 
hashes have been processed as natural language for training malware 
classification models (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 2021).

Section byte hashing. Between whole-file hashes and granular compo-

ing. By splitting a Portable Executable (PE) file and generating a 
similarity digest for each structural section (e.g. .text, .data), 
analysts can better withstand trivial obfuscations (like appended data) 
that would disrupt whole-file hashes. Shiel and O’Shaughnessy (2019) 
demonstrated that section-level hashing yields more true positives for 
variant classification than file-level equivalents. This methodology also 
proved effective in identifying variants of the HermeticWiper mal-
ware (Snoke and Jacobs, 2023). These techniques nonetheless remain 
ill-suited for the scope of this systematisation. Evaluating the binary (or 
its macroscopic sections) as a whole, they lack the granularity required 
to answer the core attribution question: How can malicious code and its 
proliferation be detected and traced?
Genetic analysis. Genetic analysis treats software development as an 
evolutionary process. Pfeffer et al. (2012) introduced this paradigm 
with MAAGI, combining static byte features (L0), call graph analysis 
(L3), and Cuckoo dynamic execution traces (L5) to cluster and recover 
lineage graphs of malware samples.
Ding et al. (2017) refined this at instruction granularity: MGeT
runs samples inside a distributed hardware virtualisation environment 
and extracts instruction behaviour sequences (L5), then identifies dis-
criminative sub-sequences as malware genes for family classification. 
Commercial platforms such as Intezer (Intezer, 2021) have since 
followed this approach, matching function-level code against a genome 
database of known malware to attribute samples to threat actors with-
out relying on whole-file analysis. Establishing authorship, therefore, 
requires moving beyond file and section-level hashes to function-level 
analysis.
4.3.2. Function fingerprints streamline forensic triage
Function byte hashing. Function-level byte hashing has been adopted in 
the industry for binary code clone detection and to bypass redundant 
reverse-engineering. Namely, FCatalog (xorpd, 2026) compresses 
shingles from L0 function byte sequences into 16 MinHash permuta-
tions (Broder et al., 1998). This normalisation enables fuzzy matching 
of known functions where code may have been slightly altered or 
shuffled.
To find exact matches in cross-compiled code, industrial frameworks 
such as BinDiff, IDA Pro, and Pharos apply hashing to Position In-
dependent Code (PIC) signatures. This approach masks volatile memory 
addresses and relative offsets in CALL or JMP instructions in bytecode 
(L0–L1), allowing for exact matches even when code is recompiled with 
different binary addresses (Schwartz, 2024). However, experimental 
evaluations suggest a syntactic limit to byte-level analysis: even with 
optimal masking, instruction-level hashes struggle to identify equiva-
lent functions when confronted with different compiler toolchains or 
optimisation levels.
Topological hashing. To overcome instruction mutations, analysts eval-
uate local Topology (L2). A prominent example is Machoc (or Ma-
choke), which generates a hash signature based on the graph layout 
to provide stability even with diverging syntax (ANSSI, 2015).
Partitioned hashing. CCBHash (Onieva et al., 2024), integrated into 
frameworks such as r2inspect, generates a one-shot composite func-
tion signature by combining features across L1–L4. For file-level mal-
ware clustering, Onieva et al. (2024) apply this function hash asymmet-
rically. Given two binaries 𝐴 and 𝐵 where 𝐵 contains 𝑘 > 𝑛 functions, 
each function in 𝐵 is compared against all 𝑛 functions in 𝐴 and assigned 
the highest resulting similarity score. Averaging these 𝑘 scores yields 
one file-level similarity value.
Because the comparison is 𝑘-to-𝑛 and the direction is determined 
by function count, the score is not symmetric: computing (𝐵, 𝐴) can 
yield a different value from (𝐴, 𝐵). When functions are filtered before 
comparison, this asymmetry compounds further, as the filtered set 
and its ordering affect the outcome. The technique is therefore best 
understood as a heuristic for approximate clustering rather than a 
precise pairwise metric.
Cascading heuristics. In practice, forensic triage is often conducted 
through a pipeline of heuristics. Tools like Diaphora (Joxean, 2015) 
begin with exact raw-byte and PIC hashes.
Next, deeper analysis is performed as the divergence between sam-
ples increases, such as the function-level small primes product or 
hashes of Strongly Connected Components (SCC) in the global CG based 
on Tarjan’s algorithm (Tarjan, 1972). In case of heavily obfuscated 
code, the analysis may even decompile the function to pseudo-C and 
apply DeepToad (Joxean, 0000a, 2018), an archived SSDEEP-inspired 
library, to fuzzy-hash the resulting text.2
While hashing algorithms do not require training, most traditional 
fuzzy hashes were designed for approximate matching of string or byte

Anomaly detection. To streamline the localisation of functional com-

4.3.3. Cross-compiled malware attribution
As threat actors increasingly diversify their target environments to 
maximise their attack surface, cross-compiled analysis has emerged 
as a critical requirement for authorship attribution. This necessity is 
underscored by the pervasive reuse of the Mirai source code across 
heterogeneous IoT botnets (Anna-senpai, 2016; Gamblin, 2016; An-
tonakakis et al., 2017), alongside the recent evolution of Advanced 
Persistent Threats (APTs) deploying unified toolkits across multiple OS 
platforms (Fakterman, 2025; Nelson, 2024; Health Sector Cybersecurity 
Coordination Center (HC3), 2024).
Contextual logic. To normalise diverse architectures and extract
function-level intent, forensic analysis must ascend to the Operational 
domain (L4). A prominent example is CIMALIR (Rahat et al., 2024), 
developed specifically for clustering cross-architecture IoT malware 
samples. The tool evaluates functions primarily via Ghidra high P-
Code (L4), extracting ISA-independent features such as the number 
of conditional control transfers, function parameters, and distinct data 
types. To distinguish malware families with high code reuse, CIMALIR
additionally extracts unique printable strings via disassembly (L1) and 
refines ambiguous function matches by incorporating call graph context 
(L3), comparing each function alongside its callers and callees.
Building upon this, CIAO (Rahat et al., 2026) lifts instructions to 
ESIL (L4) to produce architecture-neutral function embeddings, then 
applies graph representation learning over a Function Call Graph (FCG) 
(L3) to separate family-specific logic from reused library code. This 
contextual approach allows analysts to isolate proprietary threat-actor 
functions from shared third-party libraries that often cause false posi-
tives in attribution.
While these methodologies normalise feature representations across 
ISAs, they remain anchored to the Logic layer (L4). As established in 
our abstraction model, L4 reconstructions can still diverge significantly 
due to variations in bitness and data models, which alter pointer sizes 
and memory layouts even when the high-level logic is identical.
To mitigate these compilation and structural variances before se-
mantic extraction, Alrabaee et al. (2020) utilised a static binary rewriter 
before lifting cross-compiled binaries to architecture-neutral IRs via 
the angr framework. By extracting normalised features, such as ACFG 
(L2), DFG and Graph of API-Keywords (L4), they subsequently applied 
Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) to generate architecture-independent 
signatures for authorship characterisation.
Software lineage. Software lineage inference treats version history as 
a graph problem: given a set of binary releases, recover the ancestral 
relationships between them. Jang et al. (2013) formalised this with
ILINE, applying binary similarity metrics across abstraction layers 
(L1–L2, with dynamic traces at L5 for malware) to infer lineage DAGs 
automatically. Evaluated on 1777 goodware (legitimate software) bi-
naries spanning 110 years of development history and 114 malware 
samples with known lineage, ILINE achieves a mean accuracy above 
84% for goodware and above 72% for malware, even without prior 
knowledge of the dataset structure.
Static binary similarity cannot always see through heavy obfus-
cation. Ming et al. (2015) address this with symbolic execution and 
constraint solving (L4–L5), identifying semantically equivalent basic 
blocks between malware samples. To make the approach tractable, they 
introduce memoization that caches previously computed equivalences, 
reducing constraint solver invocations by a factor of three to six.
Haq et al. (2018) take a different route, replaying PANDA execution 
recordings (PANDA, 0000) to dynamically unpack malware samples 
(L5), then applying function-level code hashing on the unpacked bi-
naries to trace code reuse across versions. This combination of L5 
unpacking with procedural function hashing allows them to infer the 
lineage of packed malware families across samples. They explicitly 
note, however, that cross-compiler analysis remains out of scope: dif-
ferent compilers or optimisation settings alter the extracted operational 
artefacts, causing the algorithm to fragment the lineage graph and 
overestimate the number of distinct malware versions.
Hybrid clustering. Scaling fine-grained function-level comparisons
across large datasets of binary samples remains a critical challenge. 
Cozzi et al. (2020) addressed this challenge in their monumental study 
on the genealogy of IoT malware, mapping code reuse across hundreds 
of thousands of samples. Because calculating a full similarity matrix 
using the 1-to-1 diffing tool Diaphora (Joxean, 2015) was compu-
tationally unviable, they adopted an approximate clustering strategy 
using Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs. This served 
as a scalable pre-filtering mechanism, limiting pairwise comparisons to 
the most likely nearest neighbours.
However, while the study analysed malware across multiple archi-
tectures, the authors ultimately had to segment their lineage trees by 
ISA to maintain accuracy. This leaves a gap in true cross-architecture 
lineage tracing, as the functional relationships between diverse archi-
tectures (e.g. ARM vs. MIPS) remained disjointed. The sole exception 
was their ARM lineage graph, which successfully traced the evolutionary 
path of functions compiled in both THUMB and standard ARM modes; 
for all other architectures, the lineage trees remained siloed.
The review above establishes the design space of cross-compiled 
BFSA. The next section examines the specific artefact extraction and 
hashing mechanisms used in our evaluation.
5. Binary artefacts processing and feature hashing
In this systematisation, we restrict our scope to Binary Function 
Similarity Analysis (BFSA). We refer to ‘‘artefacts’’ as the raw data 
across the full Abstraction Model, recovered by a chosen reverse-
engineering toolchain, such as Radare2 (Radare2 Team, 2024), Binary 
Ninja (Vector 35, 2026), IDA Pro (Hex-Rays, 2026), or Ghidra (National 
Security Agency, 2019). Conversely, the ‘‘features’’ are extracted from 
artefacts and preprocessed as hashable inputs for signature generation 
algorithms. The analysis algorithm and toolchain entail a compro-
mise in the coverage and accuracy of recovered artefacts (Pang et al., 
2021). Different toolchains can exhibit divergence both in the processed 
features and in the quality of generated signatures.
Processing recovered artefacts into features for subsequent hashing 
involves clearing compilation noise and transforming artefacts based on 
their morphology. This section addresses RQ3 by systematising these 
two transformation phases.

5.1.1. Scalar artefacts
Scalar artefacts are integer metrics encoding numerical charac-
teristics of a function, such as cyclomatic complexity, the function’s 
indegree/outdegree, the total number of basic blocks, and the dedicated 
stackframe size.
Because raw scalar values are highly brittle, where a minor opti-
misation might alter the instruction count, direct numeric comparisons 
often fail. To convert scalars into resilient features, algorithms employ 
quantisation strategies. For example, in the CCBHash algorithm, in-
teger features are mapped and hashed to 4-bit representations based 
on a statistical analysis of x86 malware families. Therefore, instead 
of relying on arbitrary bounds, this quantisation technique is tied to 
real-world discriminative power.
5.1.2. Sequential artefacts
Sequential artefacts are linear streams of data, spanning L0 raw byte 
streams, L1 assembly mnemonics, and L4 local variable type sequences. 
At the Surface and Syntax layers (L0/L1), hardcoded memory offsets 
and address-dependent immediate values are susceptible to relocation; 
register-held values are susceptible to spilling, causing compilers to 
substitute stack references for register operands. PIC signatures, as 
implemented in Radare2 Zignatures, counter this with bit-level masking 
of raw byte sequences, zeroing out volatile fields so that only the 
invariant opcode logic is hashed.
Compiler optimisations may also reorder sequential artefacts across 
layers. CCBHash counters this by sorting its normalised opcode type 
vocabulary (L1) and local variable type sequences (L4) by their fre-
quency of appearance, stripping away execution order. Because se-
quences are also vulnerable to local reordering, hashing them globally 
often produces brittle signatures. To build resilience, sequences are 
broken into overlapping n-grams (shingling). For example, Catalog1
extracts 4-byte sequential chunks from the raw byte stream. Given a 
sequence 𝐴, the set of shingles 𝑆(𝐴) is defined as: 
𝑆(𝐴) = { 𝐴[𝑖 ∶ 𝑖 + 4] ∣ 0 ≤ 𝑖 ≤ |𝐴| − 4 } (1)
This conversion into sets preserves similarity even when chunks of code 
have been locally reordered.
5.1.3. Graph-structured artefacts
Graph-structured artefacts capture the topological structure of a 
program, primarily represented by the CFG at L2 and the CG at L3. 
Because standard hashing algorithms require 1D inputs, graphs must 
be flattened; and because basic block addresses are compiler-assigned, 
they must be normalised before flattening; otherwise, two structurally 
identical functions at different load addresses produce different hashes.
• Node Traversal Indexing: Machoke flattens the CFG by traversing it 
in a deterministic order, assigning each basic block an ordinal index 
and serialising the result into a single-dimensional string (e.g. NUM-
BER:[c,][DST, . . . ]; ), where [c,] denotes external calls 
from that block. This erases memory layout dependencies while 
preserving structural topology.
CCBHash applies the same traversal indexing strategy while treat-
ing the CFG and CG as separate artefacts, each serialised and 
hashed independently. The [c,] notation is therefore absent from 
its CFG serialisation: inter-function call relationships are captured 
by the dedicated CG hash instead.
• Sub-Graph Canonicalisation (Graphlets): Rather than flattening the 
entire graph globally, other frameworks extract localised, con-
nected sub-graphs of size 𝑘 (graphlets).
To ensure permutation invariance, the sub-graph is mathematically 
reduced to a canonical form before hashing, making structurally 
identical sub-graphs yield the same hash regardless of how the 
disassembler orders the nodes. This is typically achieved through 
two paradigms:
– Heuristic Sorting: FunctionSimSearch sorts the sub-graph’s 
nodes based on structural properties (e.g. in-degree,
out-degree) and encodes their edge relationships into a deter-
ministic tuple or string.
– Adjacency Matrices: Rendezvous (Khoo et al., 2013) converts 
the sub-graph into a 𝑘 × 𝑘 adjacency matrix. The matrix is 
then canonicalised by computing the minimum lexicographical 
representation of all its possible permutations, generating a 
deterministic integer feature.

rithms evaluate the similarity of these extracted features (see Table  4).

6. Methodology
6.1. Ground-truth dataset construction
To evaluate the performance of fuzzy hashing across disparate archi-
tectures, we use the Dataset-1 benchmark released by Marcelli et al. 
(2022). This dataset is a recognised standard in the BFSA literature, 
both for its coverage of analysed techniques (Le and Mikolov, 2014; Xu 
et al., 2017; Eschweiler et al., 2016; Shalev and Partush, 2018; Ding 
et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019; Massarelli et al., 2022; Pei et al., 2023) 
and for its adoption in the subsequent literature for ML training and 
evaluation (Collyer et al., 2023, 2024; Saul et al., 2024).
The benchmark comprises 5489 binaries derived from seven diverse 
open-source projects. For our evaluation, we processed these binaries

using the radare2 reverse-engineering framework. To automate fea-

We distinguish between the raw artefacts extracted by the reverse-
engineering toolchain and the features ingested by the fuzzy hashing 
algorithms. A single artefact (e.g. a recovered CFG) may supply fea-
tures to multiple distinct algorithms. To standardise this extraction, we 
mapped specific radare2 commands to the abstraction layers defined 
in Section 3:
• Binary and Function Metadata (L0–L4): Extracted via the ij and
afij commands, this includes global file attributes (format, bit-
ness, hashes) and scalar function metrics (byte size, basic block 
count, instruction count).
• Relational Graphs (L2, L3): To capture structural topology, we ex-
tract the global CG using aGf and the intra-procedural CFGs using
agfj.
• Linear Function Bytes (L0): Extracted using the p8 command based 
on the function’s calculated size boundary. While faster for gen-
erating Catalog1 signatures, this start-to-end linear extraction 
may capture trailing padding or interspersed data in non-linear 
functions.
• Zignatures (L0–L4): Generated via the zg and zj commands. While 
radare2 Zignatures natively capture a vast array of metadata, we 
extract two feature subsets for our evaluation.
– First, we extract the raw bytes (L0)4 and their correspond-
ing PIC masks (L1) to isolate invariant opcodes from volatile 
addresses. We use these masked byte signatures, which are na-
tively capped at 500 bytes in radare2, to establish a baseline 
illustrating the difficulty of cross-compiled matching.
– Second, we extract high-level semantic attributes (L4), specif-
ically local variable and argument types, which are necessary 
to generate CCBHash fingerprints.
6.2. BinRankBench: BFSA evaluation framework
A significant challenge in large-scale BFSA is the immense computa-
tional cost of the initial binary analysis. To facilitate this study, we de-
veloped BinRankBench, a high-throughput, open-source framework 
designed to automate the feature extraction from reverse-engineered 
artefacts, the generation of binary function fingerprints, and their 
comparison at scale. Even though we focus on fuzzy hashes, Bin-
RankBench facilitates the evaluation of any BFSA benchmark dataset 
and algorithm not included in this study, including ML approaches.
Our framework decouples the Analysis phase from the Evaluation
phase: using Bin2ML and radare2 (with the deep analysis setting 
‘‘aaa’’), we extracted all function artefacts once and stored them for 
reuse. The raw artefacts output by Bin2ML form a data lake of TAR
and JSON files caching the extracted function artefacts for each binary. 
These fuzzy hashes resulting from these artefacts were then ingested 
into a PostgreSQL database alongside selected intermediate features 
(such as Machoke strings) and skip-logs. Because this data extrac-
tion and database ingestion pipeline requires weeks of computation, 
we open source BinRankBench alongside all the binary artefacts, 
database dump, and intermediate data used for our evaluation. The re-
leased dataset is to reproduce our findings and evaluate new algorithms 
without repeating the reverse-engineering process.

6.3. Overhauled baseline implementations
To conduct our evaluation, we developed overhauled Python imple-
mentations for the fuzzy hashing algorithms discussed in Section 5.2. 
This ensures compatibility with the latest version of radare2 and 
improves computational efficiency (e.g. FCatalog’s digest generation 
throughput shown in Table  11). We verified via extensive regression 
testing that our optimisations do not alter the generated fuzzy hashes; 
these test suites are published in our repository.
Machoke & CCBHash. While official implementations of Machoke and
CCBHash are publicly available, they are no longer compatible with 
radare2 and require live disassembly to generate signatures. We pro-
vide overhauled Python implementations generating signatures directly 
from cached data. For CCBHash, we evaluate two variants: CCBHash-
U naively assigns equal weights to all sub-hashes; CCBHash-W applies 
the feature weights from the original publication (Onieva et al., 2024), 
optimised for clustering malware families in PE binaries.
FCatalog. For FCatalog, we found the original proof-of-concept im-
plementation to be ineffective with large functions, like the ones in the
Z3 binaries, which spanned several megabytes. We provide a higher-
performance implementation using the numba CPython library, which 
substantially improves processing speed for large-scale byte shingling.
Functionsimsearch. We evaluate three FunctionSimSearch vari-
ants. FSS is Dullien’s original implementation (Dullien, 2018) with 
uniform feature weights (𝑤=1.0); the original design’s 271,653 per-
entry weights require one table lookup per hashed feature, making 
them impractical for hashing at scale. FSSM is Marcelli et al.’s adap-
tation (Marcelli et al., 2022) with uniform weights; FSSM-W uses 
their published feature-type weights assigned to mnemonics and CFG 
graphlets (𝑀=0.05, 𝐺=1.0).
FunctionSimSearch provides Python bindings to the C++ im-
plementation. To compile these bindings, it was necessary to up-
date5 the legacy code. We publish pre-compiled Python wheels for 
both implementations (Dullien, 2018; Marcelli et al., 2022) in Bin-
RankBench. The original code also relies on an obsolete disassembler 
(Dyninst), and the adaptation by Marcelli et al. (2022) depends on 
the proprietary disassembler IDA Pro. To promote reproducibility with 
free software, we developed a dedicated adapter to generate FSS hashes 
from radare2 artefacts.
 FSSM feature coverage We discovered that Marcelli et al.’s op-
timisation of the regular expression matching immediate values was 
bound to IDA Pro’s output, which reproduces ISA-specific assembly 
syntax. The updated regular expression expected disassembled instruc-
tions to follow the ARM syntax, requiring immediate values to have 
a leading ‘#’ character. This constraint undermines Marcelli et al.’s 
results: immediate values were only extracted from ARM, not from the 
other ISAs, producing inconsistent digests and scores for Function-
SimSearch.
In our evaluation, radare2 does not replicate ARM assembly syn-
tax, so FSSM and FSSM-W both run without immediate values; the 
exclusion applies uniformly across all architectures. FSS is unaffected, 
as it uses Dullien’s original implementation.

• Tier 1 (The Target Pool): A subset of Tier 2, restricted solely to 
named functions that possess cross-compiled variations, used to 
sample ground-truth positive pairs.
By making Tier 1 a subset of Tier 2, we guarantee dataset ho-
mogeneity; a function that is eligible to be evaluated as a positive 
match is drawn from the exact same underlying distribution as the 
functions used to generate negative distractors. To populate these pools, 
we applied a multi-stage filtration pipeline to the raw output of our
radare2 extraction, intentionally prioritising dataset purity over raw 
volume (see Table  5 and Table  6).
Universal filters (applied to Tier 1 and Tier 2). The following filters were 
applied to the monolithic dataset to establish the baseline Tier 2 pool:
• Structural Qualification (Complexity Baseline): We parsed the 
initial pool of recovered functions and immediately discarded those 
containing fewer than five basic blocks (𝑁𝑏𝑏𝑠 < 5). This strips 
away trivial ‘‘micro-functions’’ (e.g. simple getters, setters, or inline 
assembly wrappers) that lack the structural complexity necessary 
for meaningful algorithmic comparison.
• Isolation and Code-Reuse Prevention: When evaluating a mono-
lithic, multi-project dataset, relying solely on symbol names intro-
duces a severe risk of collision. Open-source projects frequently 
vendor identical third-party libraries (e.g., zlib or OpenSSL), 
causing generic functions (e.g. deflate, main) to appear across 
entirely disparate binaries. If left unfiltered, these cross-project 
clones would be falsely labelled as negative pairs, penalising ac-
curate hashing algorithms.

To enforce ground-truth purity, we introduced a strict isolation
filter: any function symbol appearing in more than one distinct 
parent binary was categorically purged. Marcelli et al. (2022)

training, validation, and testing splits. Because we evaluate the 
dataset globally, we enforce this isolation requirement across all
binaries simultaneously to eradicate static-linking inflation.
• PIC-Hash Deduplication (Trivial Pair Removal): To prevent 
benchmark inflation, we eliminated identical machine code vari-
ations. Functions yielding identical PIC hashes were deduplicated. 
Inherently, this process also acts as a prerequisite artefact filter: 
any function lacking the necessary data to compute a valid PIC 
signature was discarded, as its uniqueness could not be verified.
This ensures the algorithms are evaluated on their ability to bridge 
non-trivial divergence. We retain PIC signature scores in the eval-
uation to highlight the complexity of each scenario: higher PIC 
recall highlights a greater proportion of quasi-trivial pairs surviving 
deduplication.
• Algorithmic Artefact Coverage: Finally, the functions were con-
strained to those where all necessary baseline artefacts (L0–L4) 
could be successfully recovered for all four evaluated algorithms, 
ensuring a level playing field.
Target-specific filter (applied to Tier 1 only). Once the Tier 2 universal

• Persistence Thresholding: To formulate a positive pair, a target 
function must be successfully compiled and recovered in multiple 
configurations. We dropped functions that appeared in only one 
compilation variant, as they offer no cross-compiled counterpart 
for comparison. To qualify for Tier 1, functions were required to 
possess at least two distinct intra-binary variations.

7. Evaluation
7.1. Compilation gap robustness
This section addresses RQ4 by measuring the robustness of binary-
function fuzzy hashes across the compilation gap. Therefore, we struc-
ture our evaluation around 1-to-N search and 1-to-1 similarity approx-
imation tasks (see Table  7). To demonstrate the scalability of fuzzy 
hashes, we expand our robustness evaluation to ten times the volume 
of the original ML benchmark established by Marcelli et al. (2022), 
utilising the millions of isolated functions preserved by our filtration 
pipeline.
7.1.1. Cross-compilation scenarios
To map how different toolchains and environmental factors degrade 
fuzzy hash reliability, we reproduce Marcelli et al.’s configuration 
matrix based on five variables: architecture family, bitness, compiler, 
compiler version, and optimisation level. Additionally, we reproduce 
distinct evaluation scenarios tailored to our two evaluation phases.
For the 1-to-N Ranking phase, we recreate and expand upon the ag-
gregated configurations proposed by Marcelli et al. (2022) to simulate 
practical reverse engineering tasks:
• XA (Cross-Architecture): Varies the architecture and bitness, but 
fixes the compiler toolchain. This configuration is highly rele-
vant for IoT firmware analysis, where a unified build system 
cross-compiles identical source code for diverse hardware targets 
(e.g. ARM, MIPS, x86).
• XC (Cross-Compiler): Varies the compiler, version, and optimi-
sation, while holding the architecture and bitness constant. This 
scenario mirrors supply-chain auditing and vulnerability discovery, 
where an analyst must identify vulnerable open-source dependen-
cies (e.g. OpenSSL) that different software vendors have compiled 
using their own preferred toolchains (e.g. GCC vs. Clang).
• XC+XB (Cross-Compiler+Bitness): Extends XC by also varying bit-
ness within the same ISA family (e.g. x86 vs. x64). This is rele-
vant for legacy code migration analysis and for tracking malware 
evolution as threat actors port 32-bit payloads to 64-bit platforms.
• XO (Cross-Optimisations): Varies only the optimisation level. This 
simulates standard malware analysis and evasion tactics, where a 
threat actor modifies compilation flags (e.g. from -O0 to -O3) to 
alter the structural layout of a payload and bypass static signature 
detection, while maintaining the same architecture and toolchain.
• XA+XO (Cross-Architecture+Optimisations): Extends XA by also 
varying the optimisation level, while fixing the compiler and com-
piler version. Architecture, bitness, and optimisation all diverge. 
Marcelli et al. (2022) motivate this with vulnerability search in IoT 
firmware: a known-vulnerable function compiled for x86 must be 
found in firmware where the same vendor toolchain produced ARM
or MIPS binaries at a different optimisation level.
• XZ (Total Divergence): We introduce an additional scenario where 
every configuration variable (architecture, bitness, compiler, com-
piler version, and optimisation) must differ simultaneously. XZ is 
the union of XA (architecture and bitness divergence) and XC (com-
piler, version, and optimisation divergence), combining their indi-
vidual challenges into a single maximally adversarial test. It repre-
sents the absolute worst-case for code matching, such as attributing 
highly sophisticated, multi-platform APT campaigns.
For the 1-to-1 Equivalence phase, aggregated scenarios obscure 
the precise cause of feature degradation. We therefore generate five 
controlled scenarios (arch, bit, comp, ver, and opt) where exactly one 
variable differs, and the remaining four are fixed, plus the XZ scenario 
to show performance when all variables diverge simultaneously.
7.1.2. Search and ranking
In practice, an analyst uses a target function to query a large 
database of known software to identify potential codebase origins or 
vulnerability matches. This requires a scalable pre-filtering phase to 
rank the most likely candidates before heavier, manual analysis.
To simulate this 1-to-N triage scenario, we evaluate the algorithms 
using a 1-to-100 target-to-distractor ratio. For each of the six evalu-
ated scenarios, we sampled 14,000 target functions (positive pairs) and 
injected them into a scenario-specific haystack of 1,400,000 distractor 
functions (negative pairs). In total, we query 84,000 targets against 8.4 
million distractors across the ranking phase.
Performance in this phase is measured using standard Information 
Retrieval metrics: (a) Recall@K, evaluating if the true match appears 
within the top 𝐾 returned results, and (b) Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), 
evaluating the absolute ranking position of the first correct match.
We evaluate the algorithms for 1-to-N triage via their Recall@K 
curves (Fig.  9) and MRR@10 performance (Table  8).
To visually assist the recall analysis, the plots are divided into 
quadrants intersecting at 𝐾 = 50  and a Recall Rate of 50%. The
top-left quadrant establishes the minimum threshold for operational 
utility: an algorithm that enters this region achieves >50% recall within 
50 candidates, meaning that inspecting fewer than 50 results gives 
the analyst a better-than-even chance of recovering the true match. 
An algorithm that fails to reach this quadrant offers no meaningful 
reduction in triage effort over exhaustive search. The area under the 
recall curve within the top-left quadrant then quantifies the degree of 
that utility: a higher area indicates that correct matches surface earlier, 
compressing the candidate list further. In XO, MRR scores range from 
0.2713 to 0.6865, placing the correct match within the top 4 results on 
average across all algorithms.

7.1.3. Equivalence accuracy
Once potential candidates are retrieved, analysts must perform 1-to-
1 comparisons to verify semantic equivalence. In this phase, the rank of 
the match is irrelevant; the system must produce a binary classification 
(Match/No Match) based on a similarity threshold.
We move to a balanced 1-to-1 target-to-distractor ratio. For each
of our six isolated scenarios, we sampled 500,000 positive pairs and
500,000 negative pairs, yielding 3 million positive and 3 million neg-
ative pairs in total. We use the Area Under the Receiver Operating Char-
acteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) to evaluate thresholding in each algorithm 
on this pool.
We also use this dataset to conduct a granular ablation study on
CCBHash. For each sub-hash within the CCBHash fingerprint, we 
measure the True Positive Rate (TPR) against the False Positive Rate 
(FPR) (Youden’s J statistic) to isolate the predictive power of distinct 
feature modalities across varying cross-compiled settings (see Table 
10).
The ROC-AUC metric measures an algorithm’s aggregate ability to 
separate true positive pairs from negative pairs across all possible sim-
ilarity thresholds (see Table  9). An Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 1.0 
represents perfect classification, while 0.5 denotes random guessing.
Multi-modal superiority. Corroborating the ranking phase, CCBHash
establishes itself as the best candidate for reliable 1-to-1 compar-
isons. It achieves an AUC exceeding 0.86 across every isolated variable 
change, independently of feature-weight fine-tuning. Even more criti-
cally, under the XZ total divergence stress test, CCBHash-W is the only 
algorithm that maintains a functional classification threshold (0.6027), 
whereas nearly every other algorithm collapses to random chance.
The limitations of single-modality matchers. The arch isolation scenario 
exposes the limitations of byte features and rigid matching. FCatalog, 
which relies on raw byte sequences, drops to an AUC of 0.4889; mathe-
matically indistinguishable from a coin toss. Machoke, which performs 
exact matching on the CFG structure performs slightly better than 
random chance at and AUC of 0.5493. This confirms that approximate 
matching of raw bytes and exact matching of CFG topology offers no 
reliable signal across ISA shifts.

7.2. Algorithm efficiency
This section addresses RQ5 by measuring the efficiency of binary-
function fuzzy hashes following the FRASHER framework (Breitingen 
et al., 2013; Göbel et al., 2022), originally designed to benchmark tra-
ditional fuzzy hashes. However, binary code similarity extends beyond 
the design assumptions of traditional fuzzy hashing algorithms, which 
normally rely on the same reference: byte sequences.
Because the algorithms in our study draw features from a diverse 
range of binary code artefacts, such as byte sequences, assembly 
mnemonics, CFG encodings, call-graph dependencies, and data types, 
we constrain our FRASHER evaluation to showcase practical efficiency 
performance. Specifically, we report on digest byte size, hash gener-

throughput (Table  11). Similarity digest algorithms, like traditional 
hash functions, operate in two stages: digest generation from binary 
artefacts, followed by digest comparison (Martín-Pérez et al., 2021; Flynn 
and Olukoya, 2025).6

of typical function block sizes, we exclude the upper 0.5% of out-

radare2 artefacts as fixtures. We use fixtures without repeating the 
reverse engineering process to disentangle binary analysis from the 
time required for generating a digest. For each algorithm, we time the 
digest generation throughput over the entire set of fixtures (spanning 
all deciles), repeat this 50 times, and report the overall mean.
Similarly, comparison efficiency is measured against a pooled ran-
dom sample of 5000 function pairs drawn from the ranking-pair set 
(independent of the size-decile fixtures used for generation). We fetch 
and convert all digests to contiguous NumPy arrays outside the timed

all algorithms. Each algorithm then computes similarity scores in one 
vectorised pass over the entire sample. We time this pass three times 
and report throughput as the number of comparisons divided by the 
mean pass duration.
Table  11 reports on digest size, generation throughput, and pair-
wise comparison speed. Machoke produces the smallest digest and 
the fastest comparison, owing to its identity match paradigm. PIC 
signatures generate the fastest because they only require a single dic-
tionary lookup. However, they exhibit the slowest comparison times 
due to their variable size, which scales up to 500 bytes. CCBHash
has the worst generation throughput because it processes features 
from the widest breadth of artefact modalities. Namely, processing 
function call graphs is significantly slow for large binaries. Pairwise 
comparison is fast because similarity computations operate on fixed-
size vectors. FCatalog’s Numba optimisation reaches the highest 
generation throughput at the cost of a 30-second warm-up, making 
this implementation suitable for batch indexing but not for single-query 
retrieval. Conversely, its comparison throughput is similar to the one 
measured in CCBHash as it performs the same underlying operation. 
Finally, FSS shows a slightly worse generation speed compared to
FSSM and FSSM-W. We hypothesise that such a difference could be 
justified by the lack of immediate values or the improved implementa-
tion. On the other hand, the time required to calculate the Hamming 
distance primitive 𝑑 does not show a significant difference because the 
computation is identical.
8. Discussion
8.1. Limitations and threats to validity
Our experiments are affected by the following limitations and 
threats to validity:
1. The original paper extracted binary features with the proprietary 
tool IDA Pro. We intentionally decided to avoid this tool in 
favour of radare2, where official implementations of CCB-
Hash and Machoke are provided, and to promote reproducibil-
ity with free, open source software. Consequently, a direct com-
parison with Marcelli et al.’s results is not straightforward. Bi-
nary reverse engineering is inherently heuristic: different disas-
semblers make opinionated design choices that produce different 
feature sets, as analysed in detail by Pang et al. (2021). Future 
work may study this form of feature divergence not covered in 
our study.
2. Nevertheless, we still had to re-implement or overhaul most 
algorithms. While r2inspect (Rivero, 2026) generates thor-
ough fuzzy hash reports from binary executables, it could not 
extract features from this dataset of 5489 binaries within a 
reasonable time, as it did not support cached artefacts. For this 
reason we open source BinRankBench, including both the 
code repository and the dataset developed for this study, hoping 
that our efforts can advance future work in this area.
3. Cross-compiled BFSA ground truth requires source code by con-
struction: functions can only be confirmed as semantically equiv-
alent across architectures if compiled from the same source. 
Licencing restrictions preclude proprietary software, limiting 
evaluation datasets to open-source projects that can be com-
piled from scratch. This is a widely shared constraint of the 
field (Marcelli et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; 
VenkataKeerthy et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026).
4. Compiler and optimisation divergence is a stated goal of most 
algorithms included in this study (Onieva et al., 2024; Dul-
lien, 2018; Radare2 Team, 2014). Conversely, architecture di-
vergence extends beyond their original design assumptions. We 
argue that the limitations observed in our results may not apply 
to future algorithms designed specifically for this purpose.
8.2. Algorithms implementation
For this systematisation, reimplementing all fuzzy hash modules 
was unavoidable. FunctionSimSearch was originally developed 
for DynInst, a tool for reversing Windows binaries. Marcelli et al. 
extended this by introducing feature-type weights and an IDA Pro 
Python wrapper.
We developed a wrapper compatible with both radare2 pre-
processed features and live analysis. We also publish the pre-compiled 
Python wheels for the original implementation by Dullien (2018) and 
the upgraded version by Marcelli et al.. Similarly, the published repos-
itories for Machoke and CCBHash required overhauling: (a) The 
repository for Machoke was archived in an incompatible state with 
modern releases of radare2. (b) The original implementation of
CCBHash cannot parse binary data at rest and is also not up to date 
with radare2.
We acknowledge the threat of implementation drift and address 
it by releasing a regression dataset. Future work may reference this 
dataset to validate backward compatibility. Multiple upgrades of
radare2 were also released while writing this paper; future research 
may study the effect of toolchain drift.
8.3. Methodological divergence and dataset purity
While this evaluation builds on the dataset introduced by Marcelli 
et al. (2022), we note that our empirical results for baseline fuzzy 
hashes (FunctionSimSearch and FCatalog) demonstrate lower 
absolute performance than originally reported. Because our target-to-
distractor ranking ratio remains identical to the original benchmark 
(1-to-100), this variance is not a byproduct of ranking probability but 
rather a direct consequence of our strict dataset filtration pipeline.

Specifically, our evaluation framework applies function-name isola-
tion and PIC deduplication constraints before pair generation to elimi-
nate repetitive boilerplate code. Such ubiquitous functions frequently 
compile more consistently, which can inflate success rates. We argue 
that algorithms such as PIC signatures, FCatalog, and Machoke are 
likely to exhibit better accuracy in low-complexity boilerplate.
Our study on the compilation gap is not concerned with such 
functions. PIC-hash deduplication ensures that our evaluation pool is 
devoid of trivial targets and comprises truly unique distractors. Further-
more, the isolation constraint excludes generic function names, such as
entry0 or main, where the assumption of source code equality cannot 
be upheld. Please note that, while these design choices aim to improve 
experimental rigour, they also eliminate real-world code reuse arising 
from shared libraries and boilerplate, which typically aids in practical 
malware attribution and vulnerability discovery tasks. Our experiments 
intentionally favour pessimistic conditions, such that the robustness of 
fuzzy hashes across the compilation gap is measured under diverse and 
non-trivial settings.
8.4. Choice of reverse-engineering toolchain
We ran radare2 headlessly via Bin2ML to distribute the analysis 
of 5489 binaries across hundreds of nodes in a High-Performance 
Computing cluster. Prioritising freely available software also ensures 
that our work remains reproducible and openly accessible.
8.5. Compilation gap and implementation gap
Our results establish that fuzzy hashes can close the compilation 
gap without resorting to representation learning, answering the central 
question of this systematisation.
The compilation gap is the divergence between low-level represen-
tations of the same source code across different compiler toolchains, 
optimisation settings, architectures, and bitnesses. Multi-modal feature 
hashing significantly reduces this gap; our results show that CCB-
Hash achieves reliable separation across all isolated single-variable 
scenarios. Progress on this gap would directly benefit forensic tasks, 
such as malware attribution, software lineage inference, and Common 
Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE)-based vulnerability search, where 
the analyst seeks the same implementation compiled for a different 
platform.
On the other hand, implementation gap is the equivalence between 
functions that share algorithmic intent but originate from indepen-
dently written source code. No procedural hash can close this gap. A 
hash derived from byte distributions, control flow topology, or opcode 
frequencies is specific to a particular implementation; it carries no 
information about an independent counterpart that produces the same 
output. Closing this gap requires representations that abstract over 
implementation form to recognise shared intent. For example, this is 
relevant for detecting Common Weakness Enumerations (CWEs) across 
codebases, where the goal is not to find a known vulnerable function 
but to recognise a class of vulnerability in novel, independently written 
code (Ross et al., 2025).
This distinction has direct implications for ML-based BFSA research. 
Cross-compiled benchmarks, including BinRankBench, evaluate the 
compilation gap primarily. A model that outperforms fuzzy hashes 
on such benchmarks may be doing so by closing that gap more ef-
ficiently, rather than by learning implementation-independent seman-
tics. BinRankBench establishes a deterministic, training-free ceiling 
for the compilation gap. Future work claiming ML superiority on 
cross-compiled benchmarks should validate whether the improvement 
transfers to cross-implementation pairs, where procedural methods 
offer no solution.
Saul et al. (2024) demonstrate that REFuSe, a simple 1D Con-
volutional Neural Network (CNN) on raw function bytes, matches 
sophisticated ML architectures across five benchmarks. Four of the five 
establish ground truth through the same-source compilation. The fifth 
(MOTIF) uses malware family labels, where intra-family code reuse is 
structurally closer to compilation divergence than to implementation 
equivalence. None of them evaluates the cross-implementation case. 
The over-engineering conclusion is therefore scoped to the compilation 
gap, suggesting that ML complexity may be precisely what closing the 
implementation gap requires.
The partitioned architecture of CCBHash also demonstrates how 
existing, older strategies may be replicated. Although fuzzy hashing 
techniques rarely borrow components from one another, CCBHash
took the serialisation technique proposed by Machoke and applied it to 
CFGs and CGs. Future work may consider more advanced approaches, 
such as the sub-graph canonicalisation of Rendezvous (Khoo et al., 
2013). The partitioned model makes such transfers tractable and mea-
surable: replacing one partition and re-running the ablation isolates the 
effect on that modality alone. In a single-vector approach like Func-
tionSimSearch, no individual feature contribution can be evaluated 
without regenerating the entire digest.
CCBHash was originally optimised for malware clustering, where 
control-flow obfuscation and cross-optimisation robustness are the cen-
tral design targets. To our knowledge, ours is the first evaluation of
CCBHash against a synthetic, cross-compiled dataset with function-
level granularity. In their original study, Onieva et al. (2024) evaluated 
it against file-level aggregation tasks over a malware family corpus 
comprising 40 samples. Our results show that CCBHash performs best 
across optimisation settings, demonstrating that this kind of compila-
tion noise can be overcome without training. Beyond optimisations,
CCBHash performed strongly in scenarios it was not designed for, 
suggesting that robustness built for adversarial conditions (e.g. control-
flow obfuscation and cross-optimisation) transfers across divergence 
types.
9. Related work
This section underlines the contribution of our work with regard to 
existing BCSA studies and benchmark datasets.
Göbel et al. (2022) introduced FRASHER, a benchmark framework 
for generic fuzzy hashing algorithms (SSDEEP, TLSH, MRSH, SDHash). 
Built on FRASH (Breitingen et al., 2013), it added adversarial resilience 
and needle-in-the-haystack test cases. Neither framework can evaluate 
techniques that recover features beyond raw bytes. BFSA requires 
access to source code for cross-compilation, which FRASH does not 
accommodate.
Marcelli et al. (2022) was the first to effectively overcome this 
limitation with a dataset of cross-compiled open-source projects. They 
evaluated ML techniques against a baseline of FCatalog and Func-
tionSimSearch hashes. However, PIC signatures, Machoke, and
CCBHash were not included. Their fork of FunctionSimSearch
also inadvertently disabled immediate values on non-ARM architectures, 
undermining the validity of their experiments, which mixed finger-
prints from multiple architectures. Additionally, FCatalog was tested 
with 16-byte and 128-byte digests, whereas the official documentation 
specifies 64 bytes (xorpd, 2026).
Our work replicates the similarity accuracy experiments of Marcelli 
et al. (2022) and adapts the benchmark of Breitingen et al. (2013) 
to binary functions. The resulting adaptation measures efficiency and 
robustness across the compilation gap. While Marcelli et al. (2022) 
briefly evaluated baseline hashing algorithms, their primary objective 
was to establish a performance baseline for machine learning. Our 
work inverts this focus by selecting an expanded taxonomy of fuzzy 
hashing techniques. We evaluate these algorithms against Marcelli 
et al.’s Dataset-1, designed explicitly to benchmark ML models. We 
expand the evaluation scale tenfold to provide practical and statistically 
significant evidence of the efficiency and robustness of modern fuzzy 
hashing.

Recent efforts have introduced large, automated compilation frame-
works to evaluate BCSA algorithms (e.g. BinKit (Kim et al., 2023),
Assemblage (Liu et al., 2024), BinSimDB (Zuo et al., 2026), and
EXHIB (Fan et al., 2026)). BinKit cross-compiles Linux packages 
across compilers, versions, and obfuscation levels. Assemblage ex-
tends this to Windows PE binaries (Liu et al., 2024). BinSimDB
provides the most granular ground truth, pairing cross-compiled assem-
bly sequences at the basic-block level (Zuo et al., 2026). EXHIB is the 
most diversified: four of its five sub-datasets compile from source; the 
fifth uses programming-contest submissions as a functional-equivalence 
proxy (Fan et al., 2026). Such benchmarks share the same constraint: 
function-level ground truth is established by compiling open-source 
code across configurations, as licencing restrictions preclude the use 
of proprietary software.
We identified Dataset-1 as the de facto standard benchmark in 
contemporary binary similarity literature (Collyer et al., 2023; Saul 
et al., 2024; Collyer et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a).
Therefore, this dataset ensures our experiments reproduce the con-
ditions of deep representation learning benchmarks. Evaluating against 
additional sources of obfuscation using frameworks like BinKit is a 
relevant avenue for future work.
Extensive work has reviewed the field of BCSA techniques without 
narrowing their focus on function-level analysis (Haq and Caballero, 
2021; Ruan et al., 2024; Javed et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024). Due 
to the large breadth of reviewed literature, many did not report on 
experimental results (Haq and Caballero, 2021; Ruan et al., 2024; Javed 
et al., 2025). Other researchers have reviewed ML-based algorithms, 
often evaluating them in relation to novel proposals (Fu et al., 2024; 
Shi et al., 2025; Saul et al., 2024; Collyer et al., 2024).
Recent literature analysed fuzzy hashing algorithms applied to vul-
nerability discovery and malware attribution without directly address-
ing robustness to compilation noise (Prasad and Chawla, 2026; Ross 
et al., 2025). Prasad and Chawla (2026) evaluated traditional file-level 
fuzzy hashes against ML-based embeddings for malware analysis tasks. 
Ross et al. (2025) studied file-level, section-level, and function-level 
fuzzy hashes to detect malware payloads and vulnerabilities.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to systematically 
evaluate the efficiency and robustness of binary-function fuzzy hashes 
across the compilation gap.
10. Conclusion and future work
This paper systematises the applicability of fuzzy hashes for cross-
compiled Binary Function Similarity Analysis (BFSA).
We propose the Stratified Binary Abstraction Model to map the impact 
of platform divergence on binary artefacts. Next, we provide a system-
atic review of the literature, classifying existing approaches according 
to our layered model.
We release BinRankBench, the toolchain developed for this study, 
to evaluate Position Independent Code (PIC) signatures and popu-
lar fuzzy hashes, including Catalog1, FunctionSimSearch, Ma-
choke, and CCBHash, in cross-compiled settings.
We derive a ground truth dataset from Marcelli et al.’s Dataset-1
(Marcelli et al., 2022), yielding a universal pool of 4,278,822 unique, 
cross-compiled functions. For the 1-to-N ranking phase, we query 
14,000 target functions per scenario against a haystack of 1,400,000 
distractors (1:100 ratio), totalling 84,000 targets across 8.4 million 
distractor instances over six scenarios. For the 1-to-1 equivalence phase, 
we sample 500,000 positive and 500,000 negative pairs per scenario, 
yielding 3 million pairs of each class in total.
Our findings show that fuzzy hashes relying on a single feature 
modality are too brittle to withstand divergence across the compi-
lation gap. Conversely, multi-modal hashes such as FunctionSim-
Search and CCBHash achieve improved performance that can be 
reinforced with precomputed feature importance weights. Our analysis 
of CCBHash shows that encoding a wide breadth of features across 
binary abstraction layers is the driving factor for cross-compilation 
resilience. Finally, we evaluated the informedness of feature sub-hashes 
to identify which modalities drive compilation stability. To the best of 
our knowledge, we are the first to systematically study and evaluate 
binary-function fuzzy hashes in cross-compiled settings.
We identify two distinct challenges in the field of BCSA, the compi-
lation gap and the implementation gap, as described in Section 8.5. The 
compilation gap arises when identical source code is compiled into di-
vergent binary code representations. We developed BinRankBench to 
measure the efficiency and robustness of binary-function hashes across 
this gap. On the other hand, the implementation gap concerns indepen-
dent source code representations that share equal intent. Whether ML 
models genuinely learn to close the implementation gap is the primary 
open question for the field. Our work aims to assist in overcoming 
these challenges. Future work will entail identifying adequate analysis 
strategies and feature representations tailored to the intended use case.

Valerio Bucci is a PhD candidate in the School of Electronics, Electrical Engineering 
and Computer Science at Queen’s University Belfast, affiliated with the Centre for 
Secure Information Technology (CSIT). His research investigates the cross-platform 
resilience of binary code analysis techniques, with a focus on the effect of digital 
evolution on threat design for cross-platform malware detection.
Oluwafemi Olukoya is a Lecturer in the School of Electronics, Electrical Engineering 
and Computer Science at Queen’s University Belfast. His research interests include 
privacy, malware analysis, systems security and cybercrime. His current research 
focuses on mobile systems security and privacy, sustainable malware analysis and 
detection engineering, cyber-attack attribution and connecting software engineering 
with legal and regulatory requirements, as well as the socio-technical dynamics of 
the cyber environment.
Jesús Martínez del Rincón is a Professor in the School of Electronics, Electrical 
Engineering and Computer Science at Queen’s University Belfast. He received a BSc 
in Telecommunication Engineering in 2003 and was awarded a PhD in Computer 
Vision in 2008 from the University of Zaragoza for his work on the development 
of tracking algorithms for video surveillance and human motion analysis. He also 
worked as a DIRC Research Fellow at Kingston from 2009 to 2012, leading research on 
human pose estimation, before joining Queen’s as an academic. His research interests 
include security of AI, AI for security, human motion analysis, pose estimation, activity 
recognition, tracking algorithms, multi-target tracking in real time, and malware 
analysis.
